A polynomial that is zero on an open set
If a polynomial \(p\) is zero at every point of a nonempty open set, then \(p\equiv0\): all its coefficients are zero. This holds over \(\mathbb R^n\) and \(\mathbb C^n\).

Equivalently, a nonzero polynomial cannot vanish on a nonempty open set.
Classification of real rooted generating polynomials
A probability generating polynomial \(p\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}[t]\), with \(p(1)=1\), is real rooted if and only if its distribution is Poisson binomial.

Its roots \(\lambda_i\le0\) give Bernoulli success probabilities \(1/(1-\lambda_i)\), since\[p(t)=\prod_i\frac{t-\lambda_i}{1-\lambda_i}.\]
Complex conjugation and real polynomial roots
For \(z=a+ib\), \(\overline z=a-ib\) and \(z\overline z=|z|^2=a^2+b^2\).

Real coefficients imply\[p(\overline z_1,\ldots,\overline z_n)=\overline{p(z_1,\ldots,z_n)}.\]Thus conjugating all coordinates together takes a zero to another zero. In one variable, nonreal roots occur in conjugate pairs.
Differentiation and real rootedness
If \(p\) is real rooted and nonconstant, then \(p'\) is real rooted and interlaces \(p\).

Rolle's theorem gives a root between each pair of distinct roots, and a root of multiplicity \(m\) contributes multiplicity \(m-1\) to \(p'\). These account for all \(\deg(p)-1\) roots.
Elementary symmetric polynomial
For \(0\le k\le n\),\[e_k(z_1,\ldots,z_n)=\sum_{\substack{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\|S|=k}}\prod_{j\in S}z_j.\]The polynomial \(e_k\) is homogeneous, multi-affine, and real stable. Dividing by \(\binom nk\) gives the generating polynomial of a uniform \(k\)-element subset.
Fundamental theorem of algebra
A nonzero univariate polynomial of degree \(d\) over \(\mathbb{C}\) has exactly \(d\) roots counted with multiplicity. Thus\[p(t)=a_d\prod_{i=1}^d(t-\lambda_i).\]A nonzero univariate polynomial has only finitely many roots.
Generating polynomial of a distribution
For a finitely supported random variable \(X\) taking values in \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}\),\[p_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[t^X]=\sum_{k\ge0}\mathbb{P}[X=k]t^k.\]The coefficient of \(t^k\) is the probability of obtaining \(k\).
Generating polynomial of a distribution over subsets
For a distribution \(\mu\) over subsets of a finite set \(E\), we create a varaiable for each \(e \in E\) and for \(x \in \mathbb{C}^E\):\[p_\mu(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq E}\mu(S)\prod_{e\in S}x_e.\]In general for a distribution \(\mu:\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^n \to [0,1]\) we can write:
\[p_\mu(x) = \sum_{\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}^n} \mu(\alpha)x^\alpha\]Where \(x^\alpha = \prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{\alpha_i}\). 
Generating polynomial of a sum of independent random variables
If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent nonnegative integer-valued random variables with finite support, then\[p_{X+Y}(t)=p_X(t)p_Y(t).\]Indeed, \(t^{X+Y}=t^Xt^Y\), and independence lets us factor the expectation.
Heilmann-Lieb theorem and proof idea
The theorem states that the matching generating polynomial of any graph with nonnegative edge weights is real rooted. All of its roots are strictly negative, since its coefficients are nonnegative and its constant coefficient is 1.

To prove this from the basics, we used an inductive definition of the matching polynomial:
\[p_G(t) = p_{G \smallsetminus \{v\}}(t) + t\sum_{u \sim v} w_{\{u,v\}} p_{G \smallsetminus \{u,v\}}(t)\] We then use interlacing to show that \(p_G(t)\) is real rooted. 
Hurwitz's theorem
Let \(\Omega\subseteq\mathbb C^n\) be nonempty, open, and connected. Suppose a sequence of polynomials \(p_1,\dots,p_m\) are bounded degree, each have no zeros in \(\Omega\), and converge coefficient-wise to \(p\). Then either \(p\) has no zeros in \(\Omega\), or \(p\equiv0\).
Interlacing
Let \(p\) have real roots \(\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\). Roots are listed with multiplicity. 

If \(q\) has degree \(d-1\), let \(q\) have real roots \(\beta_1\ge\cdots\ge\beta_{d-1}\). We say \(q\) interlaces \(p\) if\[\lambda_d\le\beta_{d-1}\le\lambda_{d-1}\le\cdots\le\beta_1\le\lambda_1.\]If \(q\) has degree \(d\), let \(q\) have real roots \(\beta_1 \ge \dots \ge \beta_d\). We say \(q\) interlaces \(p\) if:
\[\beta_d \le \lambda_d \le \dots \le \beta_1 \le \lambda_1\]
Strict interlacing means every inequality is strict.
Log concavity
A nonnegative sequence \(a_0,\dots,a_d\) is log concave if\[a_i^2\ge a_{i-1}a_{i+1}\qquad(1\le i\le d-1).\]For positive entries, the ratios \(a_i/a_{i-1}\) are nonincreasing. If there are no internal zeros, this implies unimodality: once the ratios fall below 1, they stay below 1.

We say a polynomial is log concave over \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}\) if  \(\log p\) is a concave function over the positive orthant. 
Matching generating polynomial
If \(m_k\) is the number of \(k\)-edge matchings of \(G\), then\[p_G(t)=\sum_{k\ge0}m_kt^k.\]Here \(m_0=1\), \(p_G(1)\) is the total number of matchings, and \(p_G(t)/p_G(1)\) generates the size of a uniformly random matching. The degree is the number of edges in the maximum matching.
Newton's inequalities
If \(p(t)=\sum_{i=0}^d a_it^i\) is real rooted with nonnegative coefficients and degree \(d\), then its coefficients are ultra-log concave:\[\left(\frac{a_i}{\binom{d}{i}}\right)^2\ge\frac{a_{i-1}}{\binom{d}{i-1}}\frac{a_{i+1}}{\binom{d}{i+1}}\quad(1\le i\le d-1).\]The positive coefficients also form an interval: there are no internal zeros. The proof is to differentiate, invert, differentiate, and then use that the discriminant is non-negative: \(b^2-4ac\ge 0\) for the resulting real rooted quadratic \(ax^2+bx+c\).
Open set
A set \(U\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) or \(\mathbb C^n\) is open if every \(x\in U\) has a small ball around it contained in \(U\): there is an \(\varepsilon>0\) such that\[\{y:\|y-x\|<\varepsilon\}\subseteq U.\]
Poisson binomial distribution
A Poisson binomial distribution measures the number of successes in a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables with success probabilities \(p_1,\dots,p_d\). Its generating polynomial is\[\prod_{i=1}^d\bigl((1-p_i)+p_it\bigr).\]If all probabilities are the same \(p\), this is the binomial distribution \(\operatorname{Bin}(d,p)\).
Positive-line test for real stability
A real polynomial \(p\) is real stable if and only if, for every \(a\in\mathbb R_{>0}^n\) and \(b\in\mathbb R^n\),\[q(t)=p(b+ta)\]is not identically zero and is real rooted.

A zero \(z\in\mathbb H^n\) would give \(q(i)=0\) by taking \(b=\operatorname{Re}z\), \(a=\operatorname{Im}z\).
Rolle's theorem
If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \(f(a)=f(b)\), then \(f'(c)=0\) for some \(c\in(a,b)\).
Stability preservers
(1) Identifying variables: \(p(z_1,z_1,z_2,\dots,z_n)\) is real stable.

(2) Partial differentiation: \(\partial_{z_j}p\) is real stable or zero.

(3) Real specialization: if \(c\in\mathbb R\), then \(p(c,z_2,\ldots,z_n)\) is real stable or zero.

(4) Inversion: If \(p\) is real stable and has degree \(d_1\) in \(z_1\), then\[z_1^{d_1}p(-1/z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_n)\]is real stable. 


Stable and real stable polynomials
Let \(\mathbb H=\{z\in\mathbb C:\operatorname{Im}z>0\}\). A polynomial \(p\in\mathbb C[z_1,\ldots,z_n]\) is stable if \(p(z)\ne0\) whenever every coordinate lies in \(\mathbb H\). It is real stable if its coefficients are real.

For nonzero univariate real polynomials, this is equivalent to real rootedness, by conjugation. Nonzero constants are stable; the zero polynomial is not.
